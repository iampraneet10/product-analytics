What this tool does

Product analytics tools answer three everyday questions: how many people use the product, where they give up on a flow, and whether they come back. This tool answers the same three from a plain CSV of events, with no setup, no account and no tracking code. It’s a learning and prototyping tool, not a replacement for a full analytics platform.

An event is one row: one user did one action at one moment, such as signup, create_project or upgrade. Everything on the page is calculated from those rows.

How to use it

  1. Get some data in. Click “Load sample data” to try it with 1,500 made-up users, or click “Upload CSV, Excel or PDF” to use your own. You can also paste CSV text into the box at the bottom of the page.
  2. Start with Overview. Check that the user count, event count and date range look right. If they don’t, your CSV columns or dates probably need fixing before you trust anything else.
  3. Build a funnel. Open the Funnel tab, then choose the events in the order users should do them. Use “Add step” or “Remove last” to change the length, from 2 to 5 steps. Read the bars from top to bottom and check the callout for the biggest drop-off.
  4. Check retention. Open the Retention tab and pick which event counts as “active”. Choose a meaningful action such as create_project instead of any event, because opening the app once is a weak sign of value.
  5. Change one thing and compare. Try a different funnel step or a different active event, and see how the picture moves. That’s the quickest way to find what matters.

What each view tells you

Overview
Users is the count of distinct user_id values. Daily active users is how many distinct users did anything on a given day. The 7-day average smooths out weekday and weekend swings.
Funnel
A user counts toward a step only if they did it after completing the step before. Each step shows the number of users, the share of the previous step, and the width of the bar compared with step one.
Retention
Users are grouped into cohorts by the week of their first event. Week 0 is their first 7 days, week 1 is the next 7 days, and so on. Each cell is the share of the cohort active in that week. Darker means more retained.
Blank cells
A cell stays blank until every user in that cohort has had enough time to reach that week. This stops recent cohorts from looking worse than they really are.

How to read the sample data

The sample is invented for the demo. In it, about 70% of new users finish onboarding, and roughly 10% make it all the way from signup to upgrade. The funnel makes the weakest step easy to find. In the Retention tab with “any event” selected, activity falls from 100% in week 0 to around 40% in week 1, then keeps declining more slowly, which is a typical shape for a product people try and only some keep using.

Questions to ask of your own data: where do most people drop out? Are newer cohorts retained better than older ones after a change? Do users who reach a certain step come back more often?

File formats and columns

CSV, Excel (.xlsx or .xls) and PDF files all work. For Excel, the first row of a sheet must hold the column headers, and the tool uses the first sheet that has them. For PDF, the file needs a table made of selectable text with the same header row. Scanned images don’t work. If a PDF doesn’t read properly, export the table as CSV or Excel instead.

You need three columns. The names user, event_name and time also work. Timestamps should be ISO dates or date-times.

user_id,event,timestamp
u1,signup,2026-09-01T09:00:00Z
u1,create_project,2026-09-01T09:20:00Z
u2,signup,2026-09-02T14:05:00Z

Limits and privacy

It has no user properties, so it can’t split a funnel by plan or country, and it shows one funnel at a time. Very large files may be slow, since everything is processed in your browser. Your data never leaves your device: nothing is uploaded or stored. The Excel and PDF readers are small code libraries that download the first time you use them.
